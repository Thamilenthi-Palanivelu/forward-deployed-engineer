# Objection Handling Guide

> Objections are not attacks -- they are buying signals. A client who objects is a client who is engaged enough to push back. This guide prepares you for the 10 most common objections an FDE faces.

---

## How to Use This Guide

1. Read through all 10 objections and internalize the underlying concerns
2. Practice the responses out loud -- reading is not the same as speaking
3. Adapt the example responses to your specific engagement
4. Add your own objections and responses as you encounter them

## Objection Response Table

| #  | Objection | Underlying Concern | Response Strategy | Example Response |
|----|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|------------------|
| 1  | "This is too expensive." | *Budget constraints, or unclear ROI -- they do not see the value relative to the cost.* | *Reframe the cost of inaction. Quantify the problem's cost and compare it to the solution's cost.* | *"I understand budget is a concern. Let me share some numbers: the problem we identified is costing you approximately $X per year in [lost revenue / wasted labor / customer churn]. This engagement is a fraction of that. The real question is: what is the cost of not solving this for another year?"* |
| 2  | "We tried AI before and it failed." | *Fear of repeating past mistakes. They were burned and need to understand what will be different this time.* | *Acknowledge the past failure. Ask what went wrong. Explain specifically how your approach differs.* | *"That's important context -- I'd love to understand what happened. In my experience, most AI projects fail for one of three reasons: unclear problem definition, poor data quality, or trying to boil the ocean. That's exactly why we start with a tightly scoped POC with clear success criteria. If it doesn't work in 3 weeks, you'll know quickly and at low cost."* |
| 3  | "Our data isn't ready." | *Fear that messy data will doom the project. May also be an excuse to delay.* | *Validate their concern, then explain that perfect data is not a prerequisite for a POC. Offer to assess data quality as a first step.* | *"You're right to think about data quality -- it matters. But here's the thing: data is never 'ready.' If we wait for perfect data, we'll wait forever. What I'd suggest is that we use the first few days of the POC to assess your data and understand what's usable today. Often, 70% of the data is good enough to prove the concept. We can identify and address the gaps as part of the project."* |
| 4  | "Can't we just use ChatGPT?" | *They do not understand the difference between a general-purpose tool and a custom solution. They may also be testing whether you are adding real value.* | *Acknowledge ChatGPT's strengths, then explain the limitations for their specific use case: data privacy, accuracy requirements, integration with their systems, reliability.* | *"ChatGPT is impressive for general tasks. For your use case, though, there are a few gaps: it can't access your internal systems in real time, it doesn't know your specific business rules, and when it makes a mistake, there's no audit trail or accountability. What we're building is purpose-built for your workflow -- it connects to your data, follows your rules, and gives your team a reliable tool they can trust daily."* |
| 5  | "How do we know it's accurate?" | *Trust and reliability concerns. They need confidence that the system will not produce wrong answers in front of their customers or executives.* | *Explain your accuracy measurement approach: benchmarks, human review loops, confidence scores, and gradual rollout.* | *"Great question -- accuracy is our top priority too. Here's how we approach it: First, we define accuracy metrics and test against known-good data. Second, we build in confidence scores so the system flags when it's uncertain. Third, we keep humans in the loop for the POC -- no decision is fully automated until we've validated the results. I'll share our accuracy metrics with you every week."* |
| 6  | "We don't have the internal resources to support this." | *Worried about the burden on their team. May have been burned by vendors who required heavy client involvement.* | *Be specific about what you need from them (and what you do not). Minimize their lift.* | *"I've designed this engagement to be as low-lift as possible for your team. Here's specifically what I need: API credentials (one-time), a 30-minute weekly check-in, and access to 2-3 end users for feedback. I handle the development, testing, and deployment. Your team's involvement is measured in hours per week, not days."* |
| 7  | "What happens when you leave?" | *Fear of vendor lock-in or dependency. They want to know the solution is sustainable without you.* | *Address the handoff plan directly: documentation, training, code ownership, and ongoing support options.* | *"This is one of the most important questions you can ask. Here's our approach: everything we build is in your codebase, on your infrastructure, using technologies your team knows. Before we wrap up, we do a full knowledge transfer -- documentation, architecture walkthrough, and hands-on training. The goal is that your team can maintain and extend this without us. If you want ongoing support, we can discuss that, but you will never be dependent on us."* |
| 8  | "Our competitor already has this." | *Urgency mixed with anxiety. They feel behind and may be impulsive or, conversely, paralyzed.* | *Use it as motivation, not panic. Acknowledge the competitive pressure and position your solution as a strategic response.* | *"That actually strengthens the case for moving quickly. The good news is that being second has advantages -- we can learn from what works in the market and build something tailored to your specific operations. And because we're starting with a focused POC, you can have a working solution in weeks, not months. Let's turn that competitive pressure into a competitive advantage."* |
| 9  | "This sounds like it will take too long." | *Impatience, or past experience with projects that dragged on. They may have an internal deadline they haven't shared.* | *Share your timeline with specific milestones. Ask about their deadline and work backward from it.* | *"I hear you -- speed matters. Here's our timeline: working POC in 3 weeks, pilot with real users in week 4, go/no-go decision by week 5. Is there a specific deadline driving the urgency? If so, let me know and I'll work backward from it. I'd rather adjust scope than miss a deadline that matters to your business."* |
| 10 | "We need to get buy-in from [stakeholder]." | *They are not the decision-maker, or they need air cover. This is not an objection -- it is a process step.* | *Offer to help them build the internal case. Provide materials they can share. Offer to present directly to the stakeholder.* | *"Absolutely -- who do we need to convince, and what do they care about most? I can help you build the business case. I can put together a one-pager that covers ROI, timeline, and risk mitigation -- the things a [CTO/CFO/CEO] typically wants to see. And if it would help, I'm happy to join a call with them directly to answer questions."* |

## Objection Handling Principles

1. **Listen fully.** Do not interrupt. Let them finish. Then pause for one second before responding.
2. **Validate the concern.** "That's an important concern" or "You're right to ask that." Never dismiss.
3. **Ask before answering.** "Can you tell me more about what's behind that concern?" Often the stated objection is not the real one.
4. **Use specifics, not generalities.** Numbers, timelines, and examples beat "trust me."
5. **Know when to say "I don't know."** See the IDK Framework (see `idk-framework.md` in this folder).

---

*The best objection handlers do not memorize responses. They understand the underlying concern and address that.*
